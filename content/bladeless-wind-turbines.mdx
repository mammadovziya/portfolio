A wind harvester doesn’t need to spin. It is possible to build a flexible structure that gathers energy by moving back and forth. The more interesting problem is how much usable electrical energy it generates over time, given its cost and reliability.

## Wind can drive an oscillation

Flow around a bluff body can shed vortices alternately from opposite sides. The changing forces can make an elastically mounted structure oscillate. A generator can then resist that motion and extract electrical energy.

Vortex Bladeless describes this approach in its [technical overview](https://vortexbladeless.com/technology/). That page explains the developer’s design; it is not independent evidence that every proposed advantage has been demonstrated in commercial operation.

A useful dimensionless relation is the Strouhal number:

$$
\mathrm{St}=\frac{fD}{U}.
$$

Here $f$ is shedding frequency, $D$ is a characteristic body diameter, and $U$ is flow speed. For a roughly constant Strouhal number, shedding frequency changes with wind speed. An energy harvester must deal with that changing excitation, along with structural dynamics, damping, and the generator load.

This is more complicated than making a pole vibrate at one convenient frequency.

## Fewer rotating parts is a design choice, not a verdict

Removing a rotor changes the maintenance problem. It does not remove fatigue, moving structural elements, generator losses, foundations, weather exposure, or installation costs.

Likewise, eliminating sweeping blades removes that particular collision mechanism for wildlife. It does not establish that a device has no ecological impact. Noise, lifetime, and wildlife effects need measurements under stated operating conditions.

I would not attach a percentage saving to these claims without knowing which device, baseline, test duration, and costs were compared.

## Compare energy over a year

Peak power is only one point on a performance curve. Annual energy depends on the output across the wind speeds a site actually experiences:

$$
E_{\text{year}}\approx8760\int_0^\infty P(v)p(v)\,dv,
$$

where $P(v)$ is electrical power in kilowatts and $p(v)$ is the probability density of wind speed. The result is in kilowatt-hours, before further adjustments for downtime and other site-specific losses.

Two devices with the same rated power can produce different annual energy. For a small installation, the comparison should also include mounting, maintenance, usable space, and alternatives such as solar.

## What would persuade me?

A measured power curve, long-duration field data, a clear accounting of failures, and an independent cost comparison would be more persuasive than a rendering of a quiet rooftop.

An unfamiliar design can be worth investigating without already being cheaper, safer, or a replacement for established wind turbines. The engineering becomes interesting when the claim is specific enough to test.

## The comparison I would start with

I would compare annual energy at the same site before comparing the promises attached to two different turbine shapes. A design can look mechanically simpler and still be the weaker choice for a particular wind distribution. The comparison gets useful when both devices face the same conditions and the losses are included.
