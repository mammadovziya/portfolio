Rotate a molecule on the screen. Its coordinates change, but the scalar energy of an isolated molecule should not be affected by the coordinate system chosen. The coordinates of a force vector, on the other hand, must rotate along with the molecule.

This gives us two useful requirements for a model. Some outputs must remain constant under a transformation, while others need to change in a predictable way.

## Invariance and equivariance

A group is a collection of transformations with composition, an identity, and inverses, satisfying associativity. Rotations are one example. Permutations of atom indices are another.

Let $g\cdot x$ describe how a group element acts on an input. Invariance means

$$
f(g\cdot x)=f(x).
$$

Equivariance means

$$
f(g\cdot x)=\rho(g)f(x),
$$

where $\rho(g)$ is the corresponding action on the output. Invariance is the special case in which the output action does nothing.

Suppose atomic coordinates are column vectors $r_i\in\mathbb R^3$. A rotation $R$ and translation $t$ act by

$$
r_i\mapsto Rr_i+t,\qquad R^\top R=I,\quad\det R=1.
$$

For an isolated system without an external directional field, a scalar energy model should satisfy

$$
E(Rr_1+t,\ldots,Rr_n+t)=E(r_1,\ldots,r_n).
$$

This condition removes dependence on a coordinate convention. It does not say that rotating a molecule relative to a fixed receptor or external field changes nothing; that rotates only part of the physical system.

## Distances remove a nuisance

Pairwise distances obey the required invariance:

$$
\|Rr_i+t-(Rr_j+t)\|^2
=(r_i-r_j)^\top R^\top R(r_i-r_j)
=\|r_i-r_j\|^2.
$$

A function built only from these distances is invariant to rigid motions. It also ignores reflections. We will return to that extra symmetry shortly.

Relabelling atoms is another bookkeeping change, provided species labels, bonds, and other features move with them. A graph-level prediction should be permutation invariant. A list of atom-level predictions should reorder in the same way as the atoms.

One simple way to obtain a graph-level invariant representation is

$$
f(X)=\rho\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\phi(x_i)\right).
$$

The sum is unchanged by reordering. Its suitability and expressive power still depend on the features and function classes; writing a sum does not automatically make a useful molecular model.

## A coordinate update that respects geometry

An equivariant graph layer can update coordinates using relative displacement vectors weighted by invariant scalars:

$$
r_i'=r_i+\sum_{j\ne i}(r_i-r_j)\alpha_{ij}.
$$

Assume $\alpha_{ij}$ is computed from invariant quantities such as distances and scalar node features, with a permutation-compatible rule. Under a rigid transformation,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde r_i'
&=Rr_i+t+\sum_{j\ne i}R(r_i-r_j)\alpha_{ij}\\
&=Rr_i'+t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The algebra enforces the transformation law, independent of the learned parameter values. This kind of construction is used in [E(n)-equivariant graph neural networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09844).

Training examples still determine which interactions matter. They do not need to teach the layer that a rigid rotation should rotate the output.

## Why forces rotate when energy does not

For a differentiable energy model, define

$$
F_i(X)=-\nabla_{r_i}E(X).
$$

Write $Y=RX+t$ for the transformed configuration. Applying the chain rule to $E(Y)=E(X)$ gives

$$
R^\top\nabla_{y_i}E(Y)=\nabla_{r_i}E(X).
$$

Multiplication by $R$ yields

$$
F_i(Y)=RF_i(X).
$$

An invariant energy gives an equivariant force field. Defining forces as energy gradients also imposes a conservative field wherever the model is smooth. These structural choices are valuable in learned interatomic potentials; [NequIP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03164) is a research example using equivariant representations.

The assumptions still matter. Not every force in every physical setting is the gradient of a position-only scalar potential.

## The symmetry you did not mean to impose

Rotations with determinant one form $SO(3)$. Allow determinant minus one as well and we obtain $O(3)$, which includes reflections. A distance-only model cannot distinguish mirror-image configurations with corresponding labels because their pairwise distances match.

Consider the signed volume

$$
V=\det\begin{bmatrix}r_2-r_1&r_3-r_1&r_4-r_1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

After an orthogonal transformation $R$,

$$
V'=\det(R)V.
$$

A rotation preserves the sign. A reflection reverses it. The quantity can therefore encode handedness for an ordered set of points, although handling atom permutations correctly requires additional care.

For some targets, reflection invariance is appropriate. For a target sensitive to molecular chirality, it can erase exactly the distinction we need. The [ChIRo paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04383) explores representations for chiral-sensitive molecular learning.

This is why “more symmetry” is not automatically better. Architecture should respect transformations that preserve the task, rather than every transformation that makes the mathematics convenient.

## What remains to be learned

When $f$ is invariant, it assigns one output to every orbit

$$
[x]=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}.
$$

It is effectively a function on the quotient space $X/G$. Different coordinate descriptions of the same object no longer require independent answers.

That reduces arbitrary variation. It does not remove the need for data, guarantee accurate predictions, or justify a symmetry that the real target violates.

The question is clear: how should the output change if the input undergoes a particular transformation? Answering it lets the model use its capacity on distinctions that matter.

## The symmetry I would question

The mirror-image example is the warning I find most useful. A model can obey its symmetry constraints perfectly and still lose information the target needs. Before choosing an invariant representation, I would ask for a pair of inputs it treats as equivalent and check whether their outputs really should agree.
